The same project, in plain words

PureLand,
said plainly.

PureLand is a free, open set of tools for examining one way you handle information, such as how you use your phone, a chatbot, or a shared folder at work. It holds two questions together: does this habit give you more control over your own attention, and does it also make you easier for someone else to exploit?

This page explains the project without most of its research vocabulary. The front page and the full record on GitHub say the same things in more precise terms, and wherever this page and those documents differ, the documents are the authority.

01 / The starting point

What is an information practice?

An information practice is any regular pattern in how you take in, store, transform, or pass along information. Checking notifications before you get out of bed is one; so is drafting emails with a chatbot, coordinating a team through a group chat, recording interviews for a documentary, or saving articles you intend to read eventually.

Most of these habits were designed by someone else, or they accumulated gradually without anyone making a deliberate decision. PureLand asks you to choose one, define its boundaries, and examine it intentionally rather than out of the corner of your eye.

An example to keep in mind

Suppose you use a chatbot to help write emails at work. You paste in your notes, occasionally a coworker's message, and ask for a draft. That is a single information practice, and this page returns to it at every step of the method.

02 / The question

Control on one side, exposure on the other.

PureLand's research question is whether a way of handling information can increase a person's control over their attention without also increasing their exposure to exploitation.

Those two outcomes frequently travel together. A tool that saves you time may also collect everything you type, and an app that helps you concentrate may maintain a detailed record of your moods. PureLand keeps both sides visible, because evaluating only the benefit, or only the risk, produces an incomplete and often misleading picture.

Several terms carry most of the argument, so here is what each one means on this page:

Attention sovereignty
Practical authority over what receives your attention. You can understand what is happening, stop, continue, ask questions, make changes, or refuse, without facing an artificial penalty for doing so.
Extractability
How easily someone else can take something from you and accumulate it for their own benefit: your attention, personal data, labor, relationships, or creative expression.
Return
What comes back to the people who contributed value, in a form they themselves would recognize as meaningful.
Consent
Permission for one specific use, given with enough information to be a genuine choice, open to revision, and withdrawable within limits that were stated in advance.

The thesis contains the complete definitions and the argument that connects them.

03 / Two ways of looking

Your body, and the system.

PureLand examines a practice from two distinct angles and deliberately keeps them separate.

The first angle is internal. Before you have a name for what you are feeling, something has already occurred: a notification arrives, your chest tightens, and your hand reaches for the phone. This approach draws on Buddhist meditation practice, which pays careful attention to that first moment of contact between the senses and the world. Only you can perform this kind of observation, and no software can do it on your behalf.

The second angle is external. It studies the system itself: what it collects, what it converts that material into, who has access to the results, and who ultimately benefits. This side borrows from information systems theory, the field that studies how organizations and databases process what enters them.

The project's hypothesis is that holding both perspectives at once reveals something neither one reveals alone. That hypothesis is untested. If people try both and discover that the two views have nothing meaningful to say to each other, PureLand should abandon one of them.

04 / The method

Six steps, in order.

The method consists of six steps, completed in sequence, on one real practice of your own. You may stop at any step, and the project treats stopping as a legitimate outcome rather than a failure.

  1. I Scope

    Define what you are examining. Name the practice, describe its boundaries, and identify everyone it affects besides you. Record what you could change independently and what would require someone else's decision. Then, before you begin, write down one result that would demonstrate the method did not help, because committing to that condition in advance protects you from interpreting every outcome as success.

    In the example: the practice is drafting work emails with a chatbot. It affects you, the coworkers whose messages you paste in, and the people who receive the emails. Your employer's policy on the chatbot is not yours to change.

  2. II Attend

    Observe what happens in you. Pay attention to your own focus and physical sensations while you use the practice. PureLand provides a list of patterns drawn from Buddhist teaching, such as grasping for more, pushing experiences away, restlessness, and mental fog. These are questions for self-reflection; they are never labels for other people, and they never become scores.

    In the example: you might notice that you open the chatbot before you have decided what you actually want to say, or that you feel uneasy pasting in a coworker's words.

  3. III Read

    Determine what you can actually see. Ask four separate questions about the practice. Is it understandable? Is the information about it reachable? Is it adaptable, meaning you can change how it works? Is it traceable, meaning you can follow where material came from and where it went? Keep the four answers distinct, and never combine them into a single number.

    In the example: can you find out whether the chatbot company retains what you paste in, and can you turn that retention off?

  4. IV Trace

    Follow what leaves and what returns. Track what flows out from each person involved, where it accumulates, who receives credit, and what comes back to the original contributors. Evaluate permission, credit, and return individually rather than averaging them.

    In the example: your coworker's message leaves your inbox and arrives at a company your coworker never agreed to share it with. What returns to them? Most likely nothing.

  5. V Adapt

    Test one small, reversible change. Choose a single adjustment based on what the earlier steps revealed. Record the situation beforehand, the benefit you expect, a new problem the change could plausibly create, and a date to evaluate it. Monitor the cost of the change as carefully as its effect.

    In the example: you stop pasting other people's messages and summarize them in your own words instead, then reassess after two weeks.

  6. VI Report

    Describe what happened, and claim no more than that. Record the result, including what went wrong, what you could not measure, and where your own conclusions conflict. The report can remain private, and nothing becomes public until the permissions for sharing it have been settled.

    In the example: a private note recording what changed, what it cost, and what remains uncertain.

The full method describes each step in precise terms.

05 / Getting started

The smallest way in.

You do not need a worksheet, an account, or anyone's permission to try this on your own habits. The smallest version is a single pass through the same six steps that keeps no record at all.

  • Scope: name one habit and identify where it begins and ends.
  • Attend: notice one thing about your attention while you engage in it.
  • Read: ask what you can genuinely see about it from your position.
  • Trace: ask who benefits and who bears the cost.
  • Adapt: try one reversible change and choose a date to look again.
  • Report: keep a private note, or keep nothing.

A private run like this exists for your own benefit. It does not count as research evidence, and nobody is entitled to a report about it. Read the private path.

If you would like a written record, there is a self-guided worksheet and a version designed for working with a chatbot. In the chatbot version, the model asks the questions in order and pauses while you complete the observation step yourself.

06 / The evidence

What has been tried, and what has not.

So far the project has one partial trial run, conducted from inside the project with assistance from an AI. No independent researcher has tested the method, and the existing evidence does not demonstrate that it works.

That trial, labeled FT-001, examined Alchemy, a free browser app Isaac built for sorting through the information a person takes in. It reviewed seven of the app's public documents and identified a genuine tension: the method values being able to trace where material came from, while the app deletes material on purpose. One change resulted, a channel through which users can submit reports and corrections, and its effects are still being observed.

The trial also exposed its own limitations:

  • No person completed the observation step. An AI cannot perform it, and nothing substituted for it.
  • No second reader reviewed the analysis.
  • No one affected by the app had an opportunity to challenge the conclusions.
  • The trial cannot distinguish whether its findings came from the method or simply from someone spending a long time studying the app.

These gaps remain in the record deliberately. A missing result is reported as missing rather than replaced with an optimistic estimate. Read the evidence record.

07 / Limits

What PureLand will not do.

  • No scores. It never combines its readings into a single number or ranks anything against anything else.
  • No certificates. It cannot declare that a tool or an organization is open, fair, or safe.
  • No labels for people. The Buddhist patterns are for examining yourself, and using them to diagnose someone else is a misuse.
  • No claim to a religious tradition. The name does not mean the project belongs to Pure Land Buddhism. The ideas it borrows are one person's interpretation, and the project says so explicitly.
  • No proof yet. The tools are unvalidated, which means no one has yet demonstrated that they measure what they are intended to measure.
08 / Other people's material

Open does not mean everything is yours.

Anyone may copy and modify the tools, but that freedom applies to the tools alone. It does not extend to recordings, messages, or stories that belong to other people, and it does not extend to knowledge a community holds for itself.

Every use of someone's material requires its own separate permission: recording it, retaining it, showing it to a reviewer, publishing it, using it for research, processing it with AI, or training an AI on it. Agreeing to one of these uses is not agreement to the others. Read the rights and consent guide.

09 / Who is behind it

One maintainer, and the problem that creates.

Isaac Rubinstein maintains PureLand through his studio, Rubinstein Productions. He alone decides which changes enter the project and when a new version is released.

That concentration of authority is a genuine weakness, and the project names it openly: a set of tools about distributing control is itself controlled by one person. The remedy the project is working toward is to make it straightforward for anyone to challenge his interpretation, correct the record, or take the tools and build an independent version.

If you try the method and would like someone to read what you found, you can request one reply from Isaac, at no cost and with no obligation. If you want to run the method together on a real practice in your organization, the Field Pilot describes how that arrangement works.
