The same project, in plain words

PureLand,
said plainly.

PureLand is a set of free tools for taking a careful look at one way you handle information, like how you use your phone, a chatbot, or a shared folder at work. It asks two things at once. Does this habit give you more control over your own attention? And does it make you easier for someone else to use?

This page explains the project without the research vocabulary. The front page and the full record on GitHub say the same things in more exact terms. When this page and those documents disagree, the documents win.

01 / The starting point

What is an information practice?

An information practice is any regular way you take in, keep, change, or pass along information. Checking messages first thing in the morning is one. So is using a chatbot to draft emails, keeping a group chat for a team, filming interviews for a project, or saving every article you mean to read later.

Most of these habits were set up by someone else, or they grew without anyone deciding much. PureLand asks you to pick one, draw a line around it, and look at it on purpose.

An example to keep in mind

Say you use a chatbot to help write emails at work. You paste in notes, sometimes a coworker's message, and ask for a draft. That is one information practice. This page comes back to it at each step.

02 / The question

Control on one side, exposure on the other.

PureLand asks one research question: can a way of handling information give a person more control over their attention without also making them easier to exploit?

Those two things often move together. A tool that saves you time may also collect what you type. An app that helps you focus may keep a detailed record of your moods. PureLand keeps both sides in view, because looking at only one of them gives you half the picture.

A few words carry most of the weight here, so here is what they mean on this page:

Attention sovereignty
Having real say over what gets your attention. You can understand what is going on, stop, keep going, ask questions, change something, or say no, and you do not get punished for it.
Extractability
How easy it is for someone else to take something from you and keep it: your attention, your data, your work, your relationships, or your words.
Return
What comes back to the people who gave something, in a form they would actually call worth it.
Consent
Permission for one specific use. You understood it well enough to choose, you can change your mind, and you know the limits of taking it back.

The thesis has the full definitions and the argument behind them.

03 / Two ways of looking

Your body, and the system.

PureLand looks at a practice in two different ways, and keeps them separate.

The first way is inside you. Before you have a name for what you are feeling, something already happened: a notification lands, your chest tightens, your hand reaches for the phone. This way of looking comes from Buddhist meditation practice, which pays close attention to that first moment of contact. Only you can do this part. A computer program cannot do it for you.

The second way is outside you. It looks at the system: what it collects, what it turns that into, who can see it, and who benefits. This side borrows from people who study how organizations and databases process information.

The project's guess is that seeing both at once teaches you something neither one shows alone. That is only a guess. Nobody has tested it yet. If people try both and find the two views have nothing to say to each other, PureLand should drop one.

04 / The method

Six steps, in order.

The method has six steps. You do them in order, on one real practice of your own. You can stop at any step, and stopping does not count as failing.

  1. I Scope

    Decide what you are looking at. Name the practice, where its edges are, and who it affects besides you. Write down what you could change on your own and what would need someone else's decision. Then, before you start, write one result that would show the method did not help. Deciding that ahead of time keeps you honest.

    In the example: the practice is drafting work emails with a chatbot. It affects you, the coworkers whose messages you paste in, and whoever reads the emails. Your company's rules about the chatbot are not yours to change.

  2. II Attend

    Notice what happens in you. Pay attention to your own focus and your body while you use the practice. PureLand gives you a list of patterns to watch for, taken from Buddhist teaching, such as grasping for more, pushing things away, restlessness, or fog. These are questions to ask yourself. They are never labels for other people, and they are not scores.

    In the example: you might notice that you reach for the chatbot before you have figured out what you want to say, or that you feel uneasy pasting in a coworker's words.

  3. III Read

    Check what you can actually see. Ask four separate questions about the practice. Can you understand what it does? Can you reach the information about it? Can you change how it works? Can you trace where things came from and where they went? Keep the four answers apart. Do not add them up into one number.

    In the example: can you find out whether the chatbot company keeps what you paste in? Can you turn that off?

  4. IV Trace

    Follow what leaves and what comes back. Track what goes out from each person involved, where it ends up, who gets credit, and what returns to them. Look at permission, credit, and return one at a time.

    In the example: your coworker's message leaves your inbox and lands with a company your coworker never agreed to share it with. What comes back to them? Probably nothing.

  5. V Adapt

    Try one small change you can undo. Pick one thing to change based on what you found. Write down how things were before, what you hope the change does, what new problem it might cause, and a date to check back. Watch what it costs, too, not only whether it works.

    In the example: you stop pasting in other people's messages and summarize them in your own words instead. You check back in two weeks.

  6. VI Report

    Say what happened, and no more. Write down the result, including what went wrong, what you could not measure, and where you disagree with yourself. You can keep it private. Nothing is shared publicly unless the permissions for sharing it are cleared first.

    In the example: a note to yourself that says what changed, what it cost, and what you still do not know.

The full method explains each step in exact terms.

05 / Getting started

The smallest way in.

You do not need a worksheet, an account, or anyone's permission to try this on your own habit. The smallest version takes one pass through the same six steps and keeps no record at all.

  • Scope: name one habit and where it starts and stops.
  • Attend: notice one thing about your attention while you do it.
  • Read: ask what you can really see about it from where you stand.
  • Trace: ask who gains and who pays.
  • Adapt: try one change you can undo, and pick a day to look again.
  • Report: keep a private note, or keep nothing.

A private run like this is for you. It does not count as research evidence, and nobody is owed a report on it. Read the private path.

If you want a written record, there is a worksheet for doing it yourself and a version you can do with a chatbot. In the chatbot version, the chatbot asks the questions in order and stops to wait while you do the noticing step yourself.

06 / The evidence

What has been tried, and what has not.

So far there has been one partial practice run, and it was done from inside the project with help from an AI. Nobody independent has tested the method. The evidence does not show that it works.

That one run, called FT-001, looked at Alchemy, a free browser app Isaac built for sorting through the information you take in. It went through seven of the app's public documents. It found a real tension: the method values being able to trace where things came from, and the app deletes things on purpose. One change came out of it, a way for users to send in reports and corrections. Whether that change helped is still being watched.

The run also showed what it could not do:

  • No person did the noticing step. An AI cannot do it, and nothing replaced it.
  • No second reader checked the work.
  • No one affected by the app got to push back on the reading.
  • The run cannot tell whether its findings came from the method or just from someone spending a lot of time with the app.

Those gaps stay in the record on purpose. A missing result counts as missing. It does not get filled in with a good guess. Read the evidence record.

07 / Limits

What PureLand will not do.

  • No scores. It never adds the readings into one number or ranks anything.
  • No certificates. It cannot tell you that a tool or a company is open, fair, or safe.
  • No labels for people. The Buddhist patterns are for looking at yourself. Using them to diagnose someone else misuses them.
  • No claim to a religious tradition. The name does not mean the project belongs to Pure Land Buddhism. The ideas it borrows are one person's reading, and the project says so.
  • No proof yet. The tools are unvalidated, which means nobody has shown they measure what they are meant to.
08 / Other people's material

Open does not mean everything is yours.

Anyone can copy and change the tools. That freedom covers the tools only. It does not cover recordings, messages, or stories that belong to other people, and it does not cover knowledge a community holds for itself.

Each use of someone's material needs its own yes: recording it, keeping it, showing it to a reviewer, publishing it, using it for research, running it through an AI, or training an AI on it. Saying yes to one is not saying yes to the rest. Read the rights and consent guide.

09 / Who is behind it

One maintainer, and the problem that creates.

Isaac Rubinstein runs PureLand through his studio, Rubinstein Productions. He alone decides which changes go into the project and when a new version comes out.

That is a real weakness, and the project names it. A tool about sharing control is controlled by one person. The fix the project is working toward is to make it easy for anyone to challenge his reading of things, correct the record, or take the tools and build their own version.

If you try the method and want someone to read what you found, you can ask Isaac for one reply. It is free and you owe nothing for it. If you want to run the method together on a real practice at your work, the Field Pilot describes how that works.
