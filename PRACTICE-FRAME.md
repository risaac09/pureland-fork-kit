# Practice frame: attention, poison, hindrance, response

This is the observe station of [the journey](JOURNEY.md).

## The claim

Attention sovereignty is not a state we recover once. It is a practice of recognizing the conditions that capture attention and choosing a response that does not deepen them.

The three poisons name greed, aversion, and delusion. The five hindrances name sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt. These lists come from Buddhist traditions. PureLand uses them as prompts for self-observation, not as clinical categories, personality types, or labels for audiences.

Skillful means names adaptation fitted to conditions. It does not mean that any tactic becomes acceptable because the goal sounds compassionate. A response remains accountable to consent, consequence, and the agency of the people it touches.

## A working diagnostic

| Condition | How it may appear in an information practice | Question for the practitioner | Possible response to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greed | Accumulating reach, data, archives, or credit beyond the purpose | What am I keeping because more feels safer? | Release a reusable method; limit collection; return value |
| Aversion | Designing around enemies, outrage, deletion, or avoidance | What contact am I refusing to hold? | Slow the reaction; create a contestable boundary |
| Delusion | Treating a proxy, model, or story as the living thing | What has disappeared inside this representation? | Restore provenance; name uncertainty; invite correction |
| Sensual desire | Optimizing novelty, stimulation, and the next click | Does this invite a choice or manufacture an impulse? | Add stopping cues; remove infinite continuation |
| Ill will | Rewarding humiliation, punishment, or dehumanization | Who becomes easier to harm in this frame? | Change the incentive; repair before amplification |
| Dullness and drowsiness | Passive consumption, habitual approval, cognitive fog | What would make active attention possible here? | Shorten the encounter; ask for a decision or rest |
| Restlessness and remorse | Repeated checking, context switching, anxious replay | What unfinished loop is holding attention? | Bound the session; expose state; create closure |
| Doubt | Endless comparison that prevents a reversible step | What small test would produce evidence? | Run one bounded experiment and record the result |

These mappings are hypotheses. They should change when practice contradicts them.

## Greed and generosity

Generosity is not publishing everything. An indiscriminate release can expose people, erase context, or feed systems that never return value. Generosity here means giving in a form that increases another person's ability to choose, understand, adapt, or refuse.

For an open-source project, test generosity at four points:

- **Entry:** can someone understand the work without an existing relationship?
- **Use:** can they adapt the method without surrendering their data or attention?
- **Credit:** can origins and contributors remain visible through reuse?
- **Return:** can findings, corrections, and benefits travel back to the people who generated them?

## The practice loop

1. Name the attention event in concrete terms.
2. Notice which condition may be present in you before assigning it to the system.
3. Trace who gains attention, data, money, status, or agency.
4. Choose one bounded response.
5. Ask for the consent required by that response.
6. Observe the effect and the burden.
7. Keep the disagreement and revise the method.

This loop is complete only when someone affected by the change can contest your reading.
