# Practice frame: attention, poison, hindrance, response

This is the observe station of [the journey](JOURNEY.md).

## The claim

Control over your own attention is not a state you recover once. It is a practice: recognizing the conditions that capture attention, and choosing a response that does not deepen them.

The three poisons name greed, aversion, and delusion. The five hindrances name sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt. These lists come from Buddhist traditions. PureLand uses them as prompts for self-observation, not as clinical categories, personality types, or labels for audiences.

Skillful means names adaptation fitted to conditions. It does not mean that any tactic becomes acceptable because the goal sounds compassionate. A response stays accountable to consent, to consequences, and to the agency of the people it touches.

## The proliferation chain

Before a poison or a hindrance has a name, something simpler happens. The Madhupiṇḍika Sutta (Majjhima Nikāya 18) describes contact, feeling, perception, and thinking following one another at each sense door, then a further step: what one thinks about, one proliferates. Past that point the sutta's own grammar shifts: the person stops being the one doing the thinking and becomes the one the thinking happens to. See [PROVENANCE.md](PROVENANCE.md) for the source and the exact language.

PureLand reads this sequence as the mechanism underneath the table below. Contact with a notification, a feed, or a message is not yet a poison or a hindrance. The three poisons and five hindrances name what proliferation has already become by the time it is noticeable enough to prompt self-observation. Naming the earlier step matters for a practice aimed at attention: it points at where a response has more leverage, closer to contact, before proliferation compounds.

The Abhidhamma's model of the cognitive process adds a finer-grained picture: a seven-moment impulsion phase (javana) that ordinarily runs its course and returns the mind to rest. PureLand's reading of rumination (the loop of checking, replaying, or obsessively returning to a thread) as that same phase failing to complete and repeating instead is PureLand's own extension, not a claim the source makes. The source describes one process that completes; it does not describe looping as a distinct mechanism. Treat this reading as a hypothesis, the way the table below is a hypothesis, not as an established doctrinal claim.

**What would count against this.** If a response applied later in the chain, once a hindrance or a poison is already present, works as well as or better than a response applied earlier, at contact or feeling, then the leverage claim above fails, even if the descriptive chain itself still holds. This has not been tested. See [RESEARCH-STATUS.md](RESEARCH-STATUS.md).

## Self-observation prompts

Apply these prompts to your own participation and conditions. Do not use the table to diagnose a participant, audience, community, or system.

| Condition | How you may notice it in your information practice | Question for the practitioner | Possible response to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greed | Piling up reach, data, archives, or credit beyond the purpose | What am I keeping because more feels safer? | Release a reusable method; limit collection; return value |
| Aversion | Designing around enemies, outrage, deletion, or avoidance | What contact am I refusing to hold? | Slow the reaction; create a boundary people can challenge |
| Delusion | Treating a proxy, a model, or a story as the living thing | What has disappeared inside this representation? | Restore the trail to the source; name the uncertainty; invite correction |
| Sensual desire | Optimizing for novelty, stimulation, and the next click | Does this invite a choice or manufacture an impulse? | Add stopping cues; remove infinite continuation |
| Ill will | Rewarding humiliation, punishment, or dehumanization | Who becomes easier to harm in this frame? | Change the incentive; repair before amplifying |
| Dullness and drowsiness | Passive consumption, habitual approval, mental fog | What would make active attention possible here? | Shorten the encounter; ask for a decision or a rest |
| Restlessness and remorse | Repeated checking, constant switching, anxious replay | What unfinished loop is holding attention? | Bound the session; show the state; create closure |
| Doubt | Endless comparison that prevents even a reversible step | What small test would produce evidence? | Run one bounded experiment and record the result |

These mappings are hypotheses. They should change when practice contradicts them.

## Greed and generosity

PureLand defines generosity for this project as giving in a form that increases another person's ability to choose, understand, adapt, or refuse. This definition is original synthesis, not a definition established by the Buddhist sources in [PROVENANCE.md](PROVENANCE.md). It does not mean publishing everything. An indiscriminate release can expose people, erase context, or feed systems that never return value.

For an open-source project, test generosity at four points:

- **Entry:** can someone understand the work without already knowing you?
- **Use:** can they adapt the method without surrendering their data or attention?
- **Credit:** can the origins and the contributors stay visible through reuse?
- **Return:** can findings, corrections, and benefits travel back to the people who generated them?

## The practice loop

1. Name the attention event in concrete terms.
2. Notice which condition may be present in you, before assigning it to the system.
3. Trace who gains attention, data, money, status, or agency.
4. Choose one bounded response.
5. Ask for the consent that response requires.
6. Watch the effect and the cost.
7. Keep the disagreement and revise the method.

This loop is complete only when someone affected by the change can challenge your reading.
